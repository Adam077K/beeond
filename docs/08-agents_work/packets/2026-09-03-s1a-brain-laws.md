## Corrections to the plan's paraphrases

These are the places where §6 or §0.9 states something the source does not support.

1. **"The 50 ms judgement — visual appeal is decided before anything is read."** Half right. Lindgaard et al. measured *ratings of visual appeal* at 50 ms and 500 ms and found them highly correlated. The study did not measure reading, comprehension, credibility, trust or any behaviour. Nor did it show the judgment is fixed: "rarely changes if you give people more time" is NN/g's gloss, and the authors themselves floated a mere-exposure interpretation. Tractinsky et al. 2006 extended the result to 500 ms versus 10 s. The defensible statement is: *an aesthetic impression stabilises within about 50 ms and correlates strongly with the impression at longer exposures.* Not: *the visitor has decided.*

2. **"Miller's law — people hold about four things at once."** The number four is Nelson Cowan, *The magical number 4 in short-term memory*, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24:87–185 (2001), which explicitly revised Miller downward. Miller 1956 said seven plus or minus two, and was arguing about chunking and channel capacity rather than issuing a design limit. Attributing "four" to Miller merges two papers that disagree. lawsofux warns against the whole move: "Don't use the 'magical number seven' to justify unnecessary design limitations."

3. **"Doherty threshold — interaction feels fluid under roughly 400 ms."** Doherty & Thadani 1982 measured programmer productivity against mainframe terminal response times at IBM. It is not a finding about animation, scroll smoothness or web pages. Applying it to motion design borrows authority the paper does not confer. The relevant sourced number for motion is NN/g's 100–400 ms band.

4. **"F- and Z-patterns — eyes land top-left, cross, drop. Headline top-left, button on the reading path."** NN/g's position is the reverse of a prescription. The F-pattern is "the default pattern when there are no strong cues to attract the eyes towards meaningful information," and NN/g calls it bad for users and businesses, because content gets skipped on position alone. It is a symptom of missing hierarchy, not a layout to design toward. Separately, I found **no NN/g or academic source for the Z-pattern at all** — it is in GAPS.

5. **"Peripheral motion capture — movement at the edge of vision pulls attention."** Franconeri & Simons 2003 found that translating and **looming** stimuli capture attention and **receding stimuli do not**. Direction is part of the finding. A drift away from the viewer is not the same event as something approaching.

6. **"Anchoring with no price — the mind anchors on whatever number it sees first, so with no numbers the anchor is the free audit's value."** Tversky & Kahneman's anchoring heuristic concerns adjustment from a numerical anchor in numerical prediction. Extending it to say that removing all numbers makes a non-numerical thing the anchor is not in the paper, and I found no source for that extension. The decision to carry no numbers is well-founded on other grounds (`SITE-STORYBOARD.md` §0.3 item 1); this justification is not one of them.
7. **"Hick's law / choice architecture — one ask, one label."** Hick's law is solid inside its own reaction-time paradigm. The marketing corollary that fewer options convert better is contradicted by the largest available synthesis: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd 2010, 63 conditions and 5,036 participants, mean effect size "virtually zero." The one-ask decision is FOUNDER-decided and needs no evidential prop; it should not be presented as evidence-backed.

8. **"Picture superiority — pictures are remembered; sentences are not."** Standing 1973 is about **recognition memory**, and words were recognised too, just less well than pictures. Nothing in it speaks to whether a picture communicates a service proposition better than a sentence. The finding supports *memorability*, not *comprehension* or *persuasion* — and Tversky et al. 2002 (row A4) is evidence the other way on comprehension.

9. **"Vestibular / reduced motion — scroll-linked motion makes some people sick."** True, and the standard is clear, but two precisions matter. WCAG 2.3.3 is **Level AAA**, not AA, so it is above the usual conformance target. And the 35.4% figure from Agrawal et al. is the prevalence of **vestibular dysfunction measured by a postural test** in US adults 40 and over. It is not the share of people who get sick from a website, and quoting it as such would be exactly the kind of number this project strips.

10. **"Operational transparency — the site's whole thesis."** The evidence is strong and it is about something adjacent. Buell's experiments show transparency **inside a live service transaction**: a search running in front of you, a kitchen you can see into, a wait you are experiencing now. Beeond's page would *describe* a transparency that will exist later, for a service not yet purchased. I found no study on whether depicted or promised transparency produces the same reciprocity effect. That is the single largest unverified assumption in the plan, and it is in GAPS.

11. **"Zeigarnik effect" and "Von Restorff effect."** Both are 1920s–1930s findings carried into design writing largely through repetition. lawsofux gives neither a paper title. I found no modern replication for either in this sweep. They are usable as vocabulary for a design intention; they are not evidence, and the plan treats them at the same level as Fitts's law, which is a different kind of thing entirely.

## §C — Does immersion help or hurt conversion?

**The direct question has no answer in the literature I could reach.** I found no study comparing award-style, scroll-driven sites against conventional pages on lead-generation conversion, on any sample. **No evidence found.** What follows is the surrounding evidence, and it does not point the same way in every direction.

**What tests badly is the specific techniques, not immersion as an idea.** The two devices most associated with award-grade scroll experiences have both been user-tested by NN/g and both failed. Parallax: users "aren't 'wowed'," animated text scrolled past before it could be read, and NN/g concluded the benefits do not justify the effort (Sherwin, 2019-01-20, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallax-usability/). Scrolljacking: most participants were at least mildly disoriented, some read it as a bug, and it is worse on mobile for compounding reasons — longer scroll durations, smaller screens, touch-only input, harder recovery (Paul, 2023-08-06, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/). NN/g does name conditions under which scrolljacking works, and they are narrow: genuinely valuable contextual information, optimised scroll rate, away from text-heavy sections, below the fold.

**Award-winning sites have been studied once, recently, and the finding is unflattering — but I could not verify its method.** Balcerzak & Kwiatkowska, *Balancing Beauty and Usability: A Comprehensive Evaluation of Awwwards-Winning Websites*, in *Digital Interaction and Machine Intelligence* (Springer, October 2025, https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-05802-7_26). Mixed method: Nielsen heuristic analysis, eye-tracking, and user surveys. The reported finding is that "many award-winning websites exhibit a significant imbalance between aesthetic innovation and practical functionality," and that such sites "are often criticized for their low level of functionality and usability." **Confidence: medium.** The chapter is paywalled — Springer redirected my fetch to an identity provider — and I took this from the University of Warsaw lab's own summary (https://blog.lbm.uw.edu.pl/en/balancing-beauty-and-usability-a-comprehensive-evaluation-of-awwwards-winning-websites/, accessed 2026-09-03). **Neither the number of sites nor the number of participants is stated in any source I could reach.** Treat it as a signal, not a measurement.

**An award is not a usability certificate, but it is not indifferent either.** Awwwards scores Design 40%, Usability 30%, Creativity 20%, Content 10%; Site of the Day goes to the highest jury scores, with at least 18 jurors over five days and the three most deviant scores dropped (https://www.awwwards.com/about-evaluation/, accessed 2026-09-03). Usability is the second-heaviest criterion. So the award selects for something that includes usability, judged by designers, and the 2025 study above suggests that judgment and measured user performance come apart.

**Aesthetics buy three things that are measured.** Credibility: in Fogg et al.'s study of 2,684 people, "design look" was the most-cited basis for credibility judgments at 46.1%, ahead of information design at 28.5%. Tolerance: the aesthetic-usability effect means an appealing interface is forgiven its minor faults, per Kurosu & Kashimura's 252-participant study. And absorption: Green & Brock's transportation work found that highly transported readers held more story-consistent beliefs and "found fewer false notes." That last one deserves saying out loud for a company selling honesty — the mechanism by which narrative persuades is *reduced scrutiny*.

**Load time is where immersion is paid for, and it is the one place with money attached.** The Google/Deloitte study of 37 brand sites and 30+ million sessions found that a 0.1 second improvement moved lead-generation form-submission progression by 21.6% and retail conversion by 8.4% (https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions). The Core Web Vitals case-study collection at https://web.dev/case-studies/vitals-business-impact reports the same direction across many companies, for example Vodafone Italy: "improved LCP by 31% to achieve 8% more sales." For scale, the median mobile page in October 2024 weighed 2,311 KB, of which 558 KB was JavaScript (https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/page-weight). A motion-heavy build spends against a number that has a measured price.

**The implementation route matters for cost, but the published magnitude does not exist.** Chrome documents that CSS scroll-driven animations run off the main thread via the compositor, where JavaScript scroll listeners do not (https://developer.chrome.com/docs/css-ui/scroll-driven-animations). Chrome's own case study (Yuriko Hirota, updated 2023-07-12, https://developer.chrome.com/blog/scroll-animation-performance-case-study) demonstrates the difference with DevTools screenshots and the line "The CPU usage is completely different in DevTools, as shown in the following screenshots" — **it publishes no CPU percentage, frame rate or main-thread figure.** A "50% to 2% CPU" number circulates in secondary write-ups; I could not source it to the Chrome page and am not carrying it. **UNKNOWN.**

**Comprehension is the trade-off nobody advertises.** Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt's review found that when the information content is held equal, animated visualisations do not beat static ones and are sometimes worse. If a scene's job is to make a mechanism understood, motion is not, on this evidence, the thing that does it. Motion is well-evidenced for feedback, state change and continuity — the uses NN/g's duration guidance addresses — and poorly evidenced for explanation.

**Mobile.** NN/g states scrolljacking is worse on mobile and gives the reasons. **I found no quantified mobile conversion penalty for scroll-driven or animation-heavy pages.** No evidence found.

**The accessibility floor is not a trade-off, it is a constraint.** WCAG 2.3.3 (Level AAA) requires interaction-triggered motion animation to be disableable unless essential, and `prefers-reduced-motion` is the mechanism. WCAG explicitly exempts the scrolling movement itself: "Moving new content into the viewport is essential for scrolling. The user controls the essential scrolling movement so it is allowed." So the standard's target is the parallax and the scrubbed set-piece, not the scroll.

**Net, stated as findings and not as advice.** The evidence base contains no verdict on immersion versus conventional pages for lead generation. It contains a measured price for the bytes and latency immersion costs; failed user tests for the two techniques most associated with it; a review finding that animation does not teach better than statics; and measured returns for visual quality in credibility and in tolerance for faults. Whether that trade is worth making for Beeond is a decision, and decisions come back down.
